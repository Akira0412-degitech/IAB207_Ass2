Acknowledgement of Country

Every gig listed on Amplifi takes place on the lands of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. This page explains what an Acknowledgement of Country is, why it matters, how it differs from a Welcome to Country, and who the Traditional Custodians of Australia's capital cities are.

For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, Country means far more than a place on a map. It takes in the lands, waterways and seas a people belong to, together with their law, language, culture, family and identity.

Amplifi's acknowledgement

Amplifi acknowledges the Traditional Custodians of the lands on which these gigs take place, and pays respect to Elders past and present.

What is an Acknowledgement of Country?

An Acknowledgement of Country is a statement of respect for the Traditional Owners of the place where people are gathering, and for their continuing connection to that Country.

Anyone can give one, whether or not they are Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander. It is usually offered at the start of a meeting, speech or event. There is no fixed wording, so what matters is that it is sincere and that the speaker has taken the time to find out whose Country they are on.

Why it matters

An acknowledgement recognises Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples as the Traditional Owners of the land, and it is a reminder that daily life across Australia happens on their lands.

Reconciliation Australia points to a long history in which Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples were left out of Australia's history books, its flag and its anthem, and for many years its democracy. Including recognition of them in events and meetings contributes to ending that exclusion.

Acknowledgement of Country vs Welcome to Country

The two protocols are related, but they are not interchangeable. The difference is who has the authority to give them.

Welcome to Country

  • Given only by Traditional Owners of that Country, or by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people they have authorised.
  • Continues a long-standing practice of welcoming visitors and offering them safe passage.
  • Opens a formal event and may include a speech, song, dance or a smoking ceremony.

Acknowledgement of Country

  • Can be given by anyone, including non-Indigenous people and First Nations people from another Country. The City of Adelaide's protocol says this directly: non-Aboriginal people and Aboriginal people from another Country can both give one.
  • Shows respect for the Traditional Owners rather than welcoming people on their behalf.
  • Suits everyday settings such as meetings, classes, gigs and websites.

Traditional Custodians of Australian capital cities

The names below follow the wording used by each city council, government or other official body listed under Sources. Spellings vary between sources, and in some cities custodianship is described in more than one way.

  • SydneyGadigal of the Eora Nation
  • MelbourneWurundjeri Woi-wurrung and Bunurong / Boon Wurrung peoples of the Kulin
  • Brisbane (Meanjin)Turrbal and Yugara peoples
  • Perth (Boorloo)Whadjuk people of the Nyoongar nation
  • AdelaideKaurna People of the Adelaide Plains
  • HobartTasmanian Aboriginal People (Palawa) of lutruwita / Tasmania
  • DarwinLarrakia people
  • CanberraNgunnawal people

Notes on naming

  • Sydney: this is the City of Sydney's wording, which names the Gadigal as the Traditional Custodians of its local area.
  • Melbourne: the City of Melbourne names both groups. The Bunurong Land Council and the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Cultural Heritage Aboriginal Corporation talked for many years about changing the boundary between their registered areas. Both took part in good faith but did not reach agreement, and each asked the Victorian Aboriginal Heritage Council to develop a proposal. The Council's June 2021 proposal recognised the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung as the Traditional Owners of what is now the Melbourne CBD, and described Bunurong Country as marked out by the waters that run directly into Port Phillip Bay. The Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung corporation consented to the change, which was set to take effect from 1 July 2021.
  • Brisbane: the sources name the groups differently. QUT acknowledges the Turrbal and Yugara, which is the wording used in the list above, while the Office of the Queensland Parliamentary Counsel acknowledges the Jagera people and the Turrbal people. Brisbane City Council's own acknowledgement refers to the Traditional Custodians without naming a group.
  • Perth: Nyoongar is the spelling used by the City of Perth. Noongar is also widely used.
  • Hobart: the City of Hobart acknowledges the Tasmanian Aboriginal People as a whole, and separately recognises the loss of the Muwinina people of the Hobart area.
  • Canberra: the ACT Government recognises the Ngunnawal people, and its interim protocol also recognises other people and families with a traditional connection to the region, noting that those who identify as Ngambri (Kamberri) have determined they are traditional custodians.

Finding whose Country you are on

The best guides are local ones. AIATSIS suggests starting with your local council's website, then your state or territory government, and contacting the local Aboriginal land council or native title body if you are still unsure.

The AIATSIS Map of Indigenous Australia is a useful starting point, but it shows general locations only. Its boundaries are not exact, and some of what it shows is contested.
